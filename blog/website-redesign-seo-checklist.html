Website Redesign SEO Checklist: Protect What Already Works

The short answer

Keep useful URLs where possible, map every necessary change and test the migration before launch. A new design does not require a new address for every page.

A redesign can look successful in a presentation and still make it harder for customers to find you. The risky moment is often the handover between the old site and the new one: a useful service page disappears, a contact form stops delivering, or a familiar URL points somewhere unrelated.

Treat the project as a migration of a working business asset. Before discussing colours, write down what the current site already does well. That gives the design and development team something concrete to protect, and it makes post-launch conversations less dependent on impressions.

Start with an inventory

Collect current URLs, page titles, main headings and important downloads. Add the pages that receive enquiries or organic visits using your own analytics and Search Console data. If tracking is incomplete, say so; do not assume an unmeasured page has no value.

For each page, choose keep, improve, merge or retire. Record the reason and proposed destination. A short service page may need better explanation, while a detailed resource may deserve to remain largely intact. This inventory also helps find duplicated or outdated material before someone starts rebuilding it.

Build a redirect map before development ends

Google recommends mapping old URLs to appropriate destinations during a move and avoiding mass redirects to an irrelevant homepage. Where a URL changes permanently, a direct permanent redirect is generally the right approach. Keep redirect chains short and update internal links to their final destinations. Google also notes that search visibility can fluctuate while a move is processed. [1]

Give the map to whoever controls hosting. A spreadsheet is a planning document; the redirects still need to be implemented and tested. Check spelling, old campaign links and downloadable files. If no suitable replacement exists, discuss the correct retirement behaviour rather than inventing a misleading destination.

Preserve the reason people visited

A cleaner page can still answer the original question. Keep valuable service explanations, eligibility details and useful FAQs unless there is a reason to change them. If you merge pages, make sure the new page covers the topics that brought visitors to the old ones.

For example, replacing separate repair and installation pages with a short “what we do” section may remove information customers relied on. The problem is not the visual layout. It is the loss of useful content. Review the new sitemap against customer tasks before approving it.

Run a launch rehearsal

  • Open important old URLs and confirm each reaches the intended new page.
  • Check canonical URLs, page titles, indexability and the updated sitemap.
  • Submit enquiry forms and verify delivery, confirmation and spam handling.
  • Test mobile navigation, telephone links, downloads and any booking or payment flow.
  • Record a rollback plan and the person responsible for launch decisions.

Google specifically warns about accidental indexing blocks and incorrect redirects during migrations. Check the live configuration after launch; a staging checklist does not prove production is configured correctly. [1]

Watch outcomes after launch

Keep a dated record of the release and major content changes. Compare enquiries, important landing pages and crawl errors over time. Allow for ordinary demand changes and measurement differences before blaming the redesign for every movement. Investigate a broken form immediately, even if traffic looks healthy.

Can you guarantee no ranking loss?

No responsible migration plan can promise that. The goal is to reduce avoidable problems and make issues easier to find. If an agency proposes changing your domain, CMS, content and URL structure together, ask why each change is necessary and how the team will isolate problems if something goes wrong.
