Online Booking System Requirements: What to Decide Before Development

  4. Include the staff workflow in the brief

The short answer

Define the rules behind an available appointment first. Then design the customer booking journey and the staff tools around those rules.

An appointment form is easy to imagine: choose a date, enter a name and press confirm. The difficult work happens behind that form. Two people may want the same slot. A member of staff may be away. A customer may pay, close the browser and wonder whether the appointment exists.

Before commissioning an online booking system, write down how a real appointment works today. Include the exceptions your team handles by phone. Those exceptions often determine the scope more accurately than a list of screens or a reference website that looks attractive.

Define what can actually be booked

List the resources an appointment needs. A consultation may require one adviser; a workshop might need a room, equipment and an instructor. Explain whether customers choose a person or simply request the next available slot. Include appointment length, preparation time and cleanup time where relevant.

Specify opening hours, holidays, notice periods and how far ahead someone can book. A slot should not be offered just because a calendar looks empty. Write down the business rule that makes it available, then use examples to check that staff and developers interpret it the same way.

Treat calendar integration as a separate decision

Decide which system owns availability and how quickly changes need to appear elsewhere. A calendar subscription, a one-way export and a two-way integration solve different problems. Ask what happens if the external calendar cannot be reached when someone tries to confirm an appointment.

Google Calendar distinguishes recurring events, individual instances and exceptions. A changed occurrence needs to be handled in that context rather than treated as an unrelated appointment. If recurring bookings matter to your business, include examples involving changed dates and cancelled instances in the integration scope. [1]

Write the booking and payment states

A useful starting list might include requested, temporarily held, confirmed, cancelled and completed. These are proposed business states, not a universal model. Decide who can move a booking between them and what the customer sees at each point. If approval is manual, avoid sending a message that implies immediate confirmation.

Where payment is required, agree how deposits, failed payments and refunds affect availability. Test a customer returning after a payment attempt without assuming the browser tells the whole story. Customer support should be able to find the booking using a reference and explain what happened without asking for sensitive payment details.

Include the staff workflow in the brief

  • Show staff how to create a booking received by phone.
  • Define who can override availability or change an appointment.
  • Record cancellation rules and the messages customers receive.
  • Decide how reminders work and how contact preferences are respected.
  • Provide a daily view that works on the devices staff actually use.
  • Agree what to do when notifications fail or a customer enters the wrong address.

Test the journey from both sides. Ask a customer to book and reschedule, then ask a staff member to locate the appointment and explain the change history. A smooth customer form cannot compensate for an administration screen that makes routine work confusing.

Launch with a manageable service range

Begin with a representative set of services and clear operating hours. Pilot the system with staff before making it the only booking route. Keep a documented fallback for temporary outages and decide who reconciles appointments taken through another channel.

Your development brief should finish with concrete acceptance scenarios: simultaneous attempts at one slot, a cancellation near the deadline, a staff absence and a failed notification. These examples give the team something observable to deliver and help prevent booking rules from becoming expensive surprises late in the project.
