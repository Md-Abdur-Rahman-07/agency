Website Handover Checklist for Business Owners

15 November 20264 min read

On this page

  1. Confirm ownership and access
  2. Ask for an inventory with renewal dates
  3. Demonstrate editing and recovery
  4. Check the journeys that cross systems
  5. Agree what support means after launch
Explore Website Care ↗
The short answer

A complete website handover gives your business control of its accounts, a usable operating guide and a tested route to support and recovery.

Your new website is live, the final invoice has arrived and everyone is ready to move on. Before closing the project, make sure your business can actually operate what it has bought. Knowing a website address is different from controlling the domain, hosting, content and connected services behind it.

Handover is easiest when it is included in the original scope. If you are approaching launch without a checklist, use the remaining review time to agree what will be transferred, demonstrated and documented. A short walkthrough with the right records is usually more useful than a folder full of unexplained files.

Confirm ownership and access

List the domain registrar, hosting account, content management system, analytics and any other essential platforms. Record who owns each account and how your business can recover access. Where an agency manages infrastructure on your behalf, the agreement should explain the arrangement and the process for leaving it.

Avoid passing passwords around in an ordinary document. Arrange access through the platform's supported account and invitation features where available. WordPress's hardening guidance discusses account security and restricting permissions. Apply the same practical question during handover: does each person have the access needed for their role, and who removes it when that role ends? [1]

Ask for an inventory with renewal dates

A website may depend on paid themes, plugins, font licences, email services and external APIs. Request a list of those dependencies, the account responsible for each and the renewal arrangement. Clarify what remains usable if a subscription ends or an agency's shared licence no longer covers your site.

Include custom code and deployment instructions in the agreement where they are part of the deliverable. Ask where the source is stored, who can access it and what is needed to publish a change. If the site uses a hosted builder, document its export and migration limits rather than assuming it behaves like a portable code repository.

Demonstrate editing and recovery

Choose routine tasks your staff will perform: changing a service description, adding a team member or publishing an article. Have someone from your business complete those tasks during the walkthrough. Record the specific steps and any image dimensions or formatting conventions they need to follow.

Discuss backups separately from editing. Ask what is backed up, where copies are stored, how long they are retained and who can restore them. Request evidence of a restore test appropriate to the setup. The presence of a backup feature in a control panel does not explain how your team will recover from a real mistake.

Check the journeys that cross systems

  • Submit a contact enquiry and confirm the intended team receives it.
  • Check booking or checkout flows using the agreed testing process.
  • Confirm analytics ownership and the events included in the project.
  • Identify who monitors integration failures and service expiry notices.
  • Review launch redirects and any agreed content migration records.
  • Make sure public contact details and notification addresses are current.

Capture the result and owner for each check. If an issue remains open, record its impact and resolution plan instead of hiding it inside a general approval email. A shared list helps distinguish unfinished delivery work from a later request for additional functionality.

Agree what support means after launch

Clarify the duration and scope of any launch support, how to report a problem and the response expectations. Separate fixes covered by the project agreement from ongoing maintenance and new features. The aim is to prevent uncertainty when a future change needs attention.

End with a concise operating guide and a named contact for outstanding items. A professional handover should leave your business able to make routine changes, understand its dependencies and seek help with useful information. That practical independence is part of a finished website, even when you choose to keep working with the same team.
