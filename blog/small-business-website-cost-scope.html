Small Business Website Cost: What Your Quote Should Include

The short answer

Compare the work behind each quote before comparing the total. A page count alone cannot tell you what a website will cost.

Two agencies can quote very different amounts for a five-page website and both be pricing fairly. One may assume you supply finished copy and images. The other may include content planning, custom layouts, booking integration and launch support. Until those assumptions are visible, the totals tell you surprisingly little.

A useful website budget starts with what the business needs the site to do. For a consultancy, that might mean explaining three services and collecting qualified enquiries. For a training provider, it could mean taking bookings, managing course dates and sending confirmation emails. Both need a website. They need different amounts of work.

Write the job before the page list

Describe the visitor, the action you want them to take and what happens afterwards. For example: a facilities manager finds a maintenance service, checks the service area, submits a request and receives a confirmation. Your team then receives the details in the right inbox. That final handoff belongs in the scope as much as the homepage design.

Separate launch requirements from later improvements. A client portal may be useful eventually, but it should not quietly enter the price of a brochure site. Ask each supplier to quote the same launch requirements and list optional items separately. This makes comparison more useful than asking everyone for their cheapest package.

Ask for these items in writing

  • Page templates and pages to be populated, including who supplies copy and photography.
  • Mobile layouts, keyboard navigation and the devices included in testing.
  • Forms, payment tools, booking services or CRM integrations, with responsibility for third-party accounts.
  • Content editing access, training and ownership of the domain, hosting and source files.
  • Launch tasks, feedback rounds, bug-fix arrangements and exclusions.

Forms deserve more attention than they usually receive. W3C recommends meaningful labels associated with their controls. A quote that includes a contact form should also explain how errors, required fields and successful submissions will be handled. These are practical usability requirements, not decorative extras. Separate the build from running costs

Request two totals: the initial project and the recurring commitments. Hosting, domain renewal, paid software, maintenance and content changes can sit in different accounts. Ask which costs belong to you, which are passed through the agency and what happens if you move to another provider.

Create columns for setup, monthly costs, annual renewals and optional support. Use the actual numbers from each proposal. Do not treat a generic online price range as a quote for your business: delivery costs, scope and existing systems can change the estimate substantially.

Define what finished means

Acceptance criteria make delivery easier to judge. Replace “the site must work well” with tasks: a visitor can submit the form on a phone, the correct person receives it, and the visitor gets a useful confirmation. Agree who supplies test content and who approves each milestone.

A brief can fit on one page. Include the business goal, audience, must-have features, current URL, content readiness, preferred deadline and budget range. If the deadline depends on an event, say so early. An honest discussion about scope is more valuable than a promise that every idea will fit.

Do you need a custom build?

Not always. An established CMS or a carefully chosen template can suit a straightforward content site. Custom development becomes easier to justify when your workflow, data model or integration needs cannot be met cleanly with existing tools. Ask the supplier to explain that decision in terms of your operating needs, rather than a preferred framework.
