Website Not Generating Leads? Check These Seven Things

The short answer

Before redesigning everything, check whether the right visitors understand the offer, can complete the enquiry and receive a useful response.

A quiet inbox does not automatically mean your website needs a new visual identity. You may be attracting the wrong audience, describing the service too vaguely or losing enquiries after the form is submitted. Diagnose the journey before paying to rebuild every page.

Start with a specific service and a specific visitor. Imagine someone who needs that service now, has found your page and is comparing you with another provider. What information would help them decide whether to contact you? Follow that question through these seven checks.

1. Is the traffic relevant?

Review the queries and landing pages you can see in your own search and analytics tools. A popular informational article may attract readers who have no need for your service. That traffic can still be useful, but it should not be confused with people requesting proposals.

Group enquiries by service and source where your measurement allows it. A small number of relevant visitors can matter more than a larger audience with a different intention. Avoid deciding success from visits alone, particularly when the business has a long buying cycle.

2. Can a visitor explain your offer?

Read the opening paragraph without the company name. Does it identify the problem you solve, who you help and the next step? Replace phrases such as “complete digital solutions” with the specific work a customer is trying to buy. A clear description makes the rest of the page easier to evaluate.

3. Is the evidence relevant and honest?

A project example should explain the brief, your role and the work delivered. Use results only when you can substantiate them. If an example is a concept, label it. A potential client can still learn from a concept, but passing it off as client work undermines trust.

Choose evidence that matches the service page. Someone looking for a booking system needs to understand the workflow more than they need a gallery of unrelated logos. Where confidentiality limits detail, explain the work at an appropriate level without inventing numbers.

4. Can someone enquire on a phone?

Try the form with an ordinary mobile connection. Check that labels remain visible, errors are understandable and submission has an obvious outcome. W3C recommends associating descriptive labels with form controls so their purpose is available to users, including people using assistive technology. [1]

Ask only for information needed to start the conversation. A detailed procurement questionnaire may be appropriate later, but it can be a heavy first step for someone still exploring a project. Explain what happens after they send the form.

5. Does the page feel responsive?

Google's Web Vitals guidance defines good thresholds of LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP at 200 milliseconds or less and CLS at 0.1 or less, evaluated at the 75th percentile of visits. These measure loading, interaction and visual stability. [2]

Use those signals to investigate friction, not as a substitute for customer understanding. A fast page with an unclear offer can still disappoint. Review oversized media, unnecessary scripts and shifting layouts alongside the content and enquiry flow.

6. Are you measuring completed enquiries?

A button click is not necessarily a delivered message. Check the full path from submission to the business inbox or CRM. Where possible, distinguish form starts, successful submissions and qualified conversations. Keep measurement appropriate to your privacy requirements and avoid collecting unnecessary personal data.

7. What happens after contact?

Give someone ownership of new enquiries and agree a follow-up process. Check spam folders and delivery failures. If visitors receive no reply, a new homepage will not solve the underlying problem. Record where leads stop progressing, then choose the smallest change that addresses the observed issue.
