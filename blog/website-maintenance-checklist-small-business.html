Website Maintenance Checklist for Small Businesses

The short answer

A maintenance plan should prove that important tasks still work and that recovery is possible. A list of completed updates is only part of the picture.

A website can be online and still be failing your business. The homepage loads, but the enquiry email lands nowhere. A plugin update completes, but the booking calendar no longer opens on a phone. These are the kinds of problems a useful maintenance routine should catch.

The right schedule depends on what the site handles. A brochure site updated occasionally has different needs from a store taking orders throughout the day. Start with the actions you cannot afford to lose, then agree checks and response arrangements around them.

Make ownership obvious

Write down who owns the domain, hosting, email delivery, CMS updates and third-party integrations. Record renewal dates in a shared system. An agency may maintain the code without controlling your domain account, so “the developer handles everything” is not a reliable operating plan.

Keep access limited to people who need it, and review it when staff or suppliers leave. Use named accounts where the system supports them. The aim is to make an urgent task possible without searching old messages for a password or asking a former contractor to intervene.

Back up with recovery in mind

WordPress documents two parts of a typical full backup: the database and the files. A copy of the uploads folder alone does not preserve the complete site. The backup should cover the parts needed to restore the actual installation. [1]

Ask your provider to demonstrate a restore into a separate test environment. Record what was recovered, how long it took and whether forms and recent content still worked. Decide how much data loss is tolerable before choosing a backup interval. A busy store may need a tighter recovery target than a static brochure site; one universal schedule is not enough.

Use a repeatable working checklist

  • Confirm the site is reachable and review availability or certificate alerts.
  • Test a customer journey, such as sending an enquiry or completing a test booking.
  • Review available updates and assess urgent security fixes promptly.
  • Check that backups completed and investigate failed jobs.
  • Review broken links, outdated contact information and renewal notices.
  • Recheck important flows after changes, with a way to roll back if needed.

Updates should be planned around risk. A staging site helps assess changes without surprising visitors, but it is not a reason to leave a serious security issue untouched indefinitely. Agree who evaluates urgency and who can approve emergency work.

Ask for a useful monthly report

A good report explains what changed and what needs a decision. “Eight plugins updated” tells you less than “the booking flow was retested after the calendar update; the payment extension needs a licence renewal before next month.” The report should name unresolved issues and their owners.

Keep maintenance separate from new feature work in the agreement. Small content edits, emergency fixes, design requests and major integrations may be treated differently. Knowing the boundary before an incident prevents a support conversation from becoming a pricing dispute.

What should happen when something breaks?

Agree an escalation route, operating hours and an initial response target. Response and resolution are different: someone can acknowledge an issue quickly while a third-party outage takes longer to fix. Ask how progress will be communicated and what evidence is needed to close the issue.

Maintenance is easiest to evaluate when it is connected to business tasks. If your site generates enquiries, the contact flow should appear in the checklist. If it sells products, order and payment checks belong there. Choose coverage around what your customers actually do.
